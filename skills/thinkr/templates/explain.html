Gradient Descent

How a model finds the bottom of its loss: take a step downhill, look again, repeat. One number, the learning rate, decides whether it glides in, zig-zags, or flies off.

A landscape of loss

Each point on the map is one choice of two parameters. The deeper the blue, the lower the loss. The path starts at the top left and follows the slope down.

The bowl is steep in one direction and shallow in the other. The steep direction sets the speed limit: the step that is safe across the walls is far too small along the floor. This is why momentum and adaptive methods exist.

One step, slowly

Every step uses the same rule. Read the slope where you stand, then move against it, scaled by the learning rate:

$$x_{t+1} = x_t - \col{1}{\eta}\, \nabla f(x_t)$$ Start at x = 1.5 on the loss curve f(x) = x². Read the slope there: f′(1.5) = 3. Uphill is to the right. Move left by the learning rate times the slope: 0.3 × 3 = 0.9. Land at x = 0.6, lower on the curve. Repeat from here. 
def descend(grad, x, lr=0.3, steps=40):
    for _ in range(steps):
        x = x - lr * grad(x)   # one step downhill
    return x

Check yourself

On the bowl above, you raise the learning rate from 0.08 to 0.24. What happens along the steep direction?

It converges faster, with no other change It overshoots and bounces from wall to wall It stops moving

Along the steep direction the curvature is 8, so each step multiplies the error by 1 − 8η. At η = 0.24 that is −0.92: the sign flips every step, and the size shrinks only a little.

Which changes can make descent fly off instead of settling?

A learning rate above 2 ÷ the steepest curvature Running more steps Making the loss steeper with the same learning rate Starting closer to the minimum

Only the product of the learning rate and the curvature matters. More steps or a better start cannot fix a step that overshoots by more than it gains.
